# Sleep for 2 to 6 year olds (English, curated from WHO and NHS public guidance)

Parent-facing sleep guidance for the toddler and preschool years, continuing where
the infant sleep notes in this library stop. Ranges are typical, not targets — some
children need more sleep and some less. General information only.

Sources used in this file:

- WHO, *To grow up healthy, children need to sit less and play more* (2019 guidelines on physical activity, sedentary behaviour and sleep for children under 5) — https://www.who.int/news/item/24-04-2019-to-grow-up-healthy-children-need-to-sit-less-and-play-more
- NHS Derbyshire Family Health Service, *Sleep for 3–5 year olds* — https://derbyshirefamilyhealthservice.nhs.uk/our-services/0-5-years/pre-school-3-5-years/caring-your-preschooler/sleep

## Sleep 2–3 years — how much a toddler needs

WHO's guidance for children under 5 says that 1 to 2 year olds should have 11 to 14
hours of good quality sleep in each 24 hours, including naps, with regular sleep and
wake-up times. From 3 years the recommended amount drops to 10 to 13 hours, which
may include a nap, again with regular sleep and wake-up times. Most two year olds
are still taking one afternoon nap, and the total across night and nap is what
matters rather than the night alone. Regular timings do a lot of the work: going to
bed and getting up at about the same time each day helps a toddler settle. Bedtime
resistance, calling out and getting up again are ordinary at this age and are not a
sign that anything is wrong. Talk to your clinician if your child snores loudly most
nights, seems to stop breathing or gasp in their sleep, is very sleepy during the
day, or if sleep problems are affecting the whole family's wellbeing.

Source: WHO, *guidelines on physical activity, sedentary behaviour and sleep for children under 5 years of age*.

## Sleep 3–5 years — the daytime nap fades out

WHO recommends 10 to 13 hours of good quality sleep per 24 hours for 3 and 4 year
olds, which may still include a nap. NHS preschool guidance gives a more detailed
picture of how that is usually spread: around 11.5 to 12 hours at night at 3 years
plus up to about 45 minutes in the day, about 11.5 hours at night at 4 years, and
about 11 hours at night at 5 years. In other words the daytime nap normally
disappears somewhere across this stretch, and the night gets a little longer to
compensate before settling. A predictable wind-down helps: set a bedtime and stick
to the time you are aiming for your child to be in bed, keep the last part of the
evening calm, tidy the toys away, and keep the bedroom dark and cool. If your child
has dropped the nap but is falling asleep in the late afternoon, an earlier bedtime
for a few weeks is usually easier than reinstating the nap.

Source: WHO under-5 sleep guidance; NHS Derbyshire Family Health Service, *Sleep for 3–5 year olds*.

## Sleep at 5–6 years — starting school

By the time a child starts school most have given up daytime naps entirely, and NHS
preschool guidance puts the typical night at around 11 hours at 5 years. School
mornings make the wake-up time fixed, so the only thing left to adjust is bedtime —
count back from the time your child has to get up and set bedtime from there. Keep
the same routine at weekends where you can, because a large weekend shift makes
Monday morning harder. A tired school-age child does not always look sleepy: they
may be irritable, tearful or unable to concentrate instead. If your child is getting
what looks like enough sleep and is still exhausted during the day, or snores loudly
every night, raise it with your clinician rather than assuming it is the new
routine.

Source: NHS Derbyshire Family Health Service, *Sleep for 3–5 year olds*.

## Bedtime routines and screens before bed for young children

The single most useful thing for a young child's sleep is a routine that happens in
the same order at about the same time every night. NHS preschool guidance suggests
setting a bedtime and sticking to the time you want your child to be in bed, using
calm familiar steps such as a bath, a story and a warm milk drink, tidying toys away
so the room feels settled, keeping the room dark and cool, and limiting screen time
in the two hours before bed. The American Academy of Pediatrics likewise advises no
screens for the hour before bedtime and no screens in the bedroom. None of this has
to be elaborate; consistency matters far more than the particular steps. Expect the
routine to be tested — asking for one more story or one more drink is normal
boundary-testing at this age, not defiance, and a calm predictable response works
better than a new plan every night.

Source: NHS Derbyshire Family Health Service, *Sleep for 3–5 year olds*; AAP policy statement *Media and Young Minds* (Pediatrics, 2016).
