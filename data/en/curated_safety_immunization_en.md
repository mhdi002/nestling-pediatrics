# Safe sleep, injury prevention, and immunization

Parent-facing safety and prevention guidance drawn from WHO and widely adopted
national paediatric recommendations. Follow your country's immunization
schedule and your health worker's advice for your own child.

## Safe sleep for infants
Safe sleep practice substantially reduces the risk of sudden unexpected infant
death:

- place the baby **on their back** for every sleep, day and night, until 12 months
- use a firm, flat, separate sleep surface — a cot, crib, or bassinet with a
  fitted sheet
- keep the sleep space clear: no pillows, duvets, bumpers, positioners, or soft
  toys
- room-share without bed-sharing for at least the first 6 months; the baby's
  cot beside the parents' bed is safest
- avoid sofas and armchairs for sleeping with a baby, which are especially
  hazardous
- keep the baby smoke-free before and after birth
- avoid overheating: dress in one more layer than an adult would need and keep
  the head uncovered indoors
- offer breastfeeding, which is associated with reduced risk

Once a baby can roll both ways independently, they may be left in the position
they adopt, but should still be placed on the back to start.

Supervised tummy time while awake supports motor development and helps prevent
flat spots on the head.

## Choking and safe feeding
Choking is a leading cause of injury in young children:

- always supervise eating and keep the child sitting, never walking or lying
- cut round foods lengthways — grapes, cherry tomatoes, and sausage into
  quarters
- avoid whole nuts, popcorn, hard sweets, chunks of raw carrot or apple, and
  marshmallows before about 4 years
- keep small objects, button batteries, and magnets out of reach; a swallowed
  button battery is an emergency requiring immediate care
- never prop a bottle or leave a baby alone with one

Learning infant and child choking first aid is strongly recommended.

## Home and travel safety
- **Water.** Supervise closely at all times near water. Drowning is silent and
  can occur in very shallow water, including baths and buckets. Empty
  containers after use.
- **Falls.** Never leave a baby unattended on a raised surface. Use stair gates
  and window restrictors, and avoid baby walkers.
- **Burns.** Set hot water below 50 °C, turn pan handles inward, and keep hot
  drinks well away from children. Treat a burn with cool running water for
  20 minutes and seek care.
- **Poisoning.** Store medicines, cleaning products, and detergent capsules
  locked and out of sight. Keep the local poison centre number accessible.
- **Vehicles.** Use an age- and size-appropriate car seat on every journey, and
  keep children rear-facing for as long as the seat allows.
- **Sun.** Keep infants under 6 months out of direct sun; use shade, clothing,
  and hats, with sunscreen on exposed skin for older children.

## Why immunization matters and how the schedule works
Vaccines protect children during the ages when infections are most dangerous,
and protect others who cannot be vaccinated.

- follow your national schedule, which sets the ages and intervals for each
  vaccine
- WHO's Essential Programme on Immunization covers diseases including
  tuberculosis, polio, diphtheria, tetanus, pertussis, hepatitis B,
  *Haemophilus influenzae* type b, pneumococcal disease, rotavirus, and measles
- doses are timed so protection develops before the period of greatest risk;
  giving them early or skipping doses reduces protection
- a delayed schedule can usually be caught up without restarting the series —
  ask a health worker rather than abandoning remaining doses
- minor illness with or without low fever is generally not a reason to postpone

Keep the child's immunization record safe and bring it to every visit.

## After a vaccination
Common, expected reactions:

- soreness, redness, or mild swelling where the injection was given
- mild fever, irritability, drowsiness, or reduced appetite for a day or two
- with some vaccines, a mild rash a week or more later

Comfort measures: extra fluids and feeds, a cool cloth on the injection site,
and paracetamol dosed by weight if the child is uncomfortable. Routine
preventive paracetamol before vaccination is not recommended, as it may slightly
reduce the immune response.

Seek care for a high fever that persists beyond 48 hours, a convulsion,
persistent inconsolable crying for over 3 hours, or any sign of a severe
allergic reaction such as difficulty breathing or facial swelling — the latter
is a medical emergency.
