# Sunburn, burns and heat illness in children

Parent-facing first aid. Children burn faster and overheat faster than adults
because they have thinner skin and a larger surface area for their weight.
Seek medical care for anything beyond a small, mild burn, and always for a
baby under 1 year.

## Sunburn — how to treat it
Sunburn keeps developing for up to 24 hours, so skin that looks mildly pink
can become considerably worse overnight.

What to do:

- get the child out of the sun straight away, and keep the burnt skin covered
  or fully shaded until it has healed
- cool the skin with a cool (not cold) bath or shower, or cool damp cloths,
  for 10–15 minutes, repeated several times a day
- apply a plain, fragrance-free moisturiser or aloe vera gel while the skin is
  still slightly damp
- offer extra fluids — breast milk, formula, or water depending on age.
  Sunburn draws fluid to the skin and is dehydrating
- give paracetamol or ibuprofen dosed by weight if the child is uncomfortable
- dress the child in loose, soft clothing over the area

What to avoid:

- do not apply butter, oil, toothpaste, or home remedies
- do not use anaesthetic creams ending in "-caine" (such as benzocaine), which
  can irritate and occasionally cause allergic reactions
- do not deliberately burst blisters; they protect the skin underneath
- do not peel loose skin off
- do not return to the sun while the skin is still red or peeling

Seek medical care for sunburn if:

- the child is **under 1 year old** — any sunburn warrants advice
- blistering is extensive, or covers the face, hands, or genitals
- there is fever, chills, severe pain, headache, dizziness, confusion,
  nausea or vomiting, which suggest sun poisoning or heat illness
- the child is unusually drowsy, floppy, or difficult to rouse
- signs of infection appear later: increasing pain, swelling, pus, red streaks

## Preventing sunburn
- keep babies under 6 months out of direct sunlight entirely; use shade,
  a wide-brimmed hat, and lightweight clothing that covers arms and legs
- avoid direct sun in the middle of the day when it is strongest
- for children over 6 months, use a broad-spectrum sunscreen on skin that
  clothing does not cover, and reapply regularly and after swimming or towelling
- remember that sunburn happens on cloudy days and is reflected off water,
  sand and snow
- sunglasses and a hat protect the eyes and face

## Heat exhaustion and heatstroke
Overheating is a greater risk for young children than for adults, and can
accompany sunburn.

**Heat exhaustion** — heavy sweating, pale clammy skin, tiredness, dizziness,
headache, nausea, irritability, and intense thirst. Move the child somewhere
cool, remove excess clothing, give fluids in small frequent sips, and cool the
skin with damp cloths. They should improve within about 30 minutes.

**Heatstroke is a medical emergency.** Call emergency services immediately if a
child has hot skin that may be dry or still sweaty, a very high temperature,
confusion or unusual behaviour, rapid breathing, seizures, or is unresponsive.
While waiting for help, move them somewhere cool, remove clothing, and cool
them with water and fanning.

Never leave a child in a parked vehicle, even briefly with a window open —
the interior heats to dangerous temperatures within minutes.

## Thermal burns and scalds
Most childhood burns are scalds from hot drinks, bath water, or cooking.

Immediate first aid:

- remove the child from the source of heat
- cool the burn under **cool running water for 20 minutes** — this is
  effective for up to 3 hours after the burn and reduces its depth
- remove clothing and jewellery near the burn unless it is stuck to the skin;
  never pull off anything that has adhered
- after cooling, cover loosely with cling film laid over the burn (not wrapped
  tightly around a limb) or a clean, non-fluffy cloth
- keep the rest of the child warm, as prolonged cooling can drop body
  temperature, especially in babies
- give paracetamol or ibuprofen for pain

Do not use ice, iced water, butter, oils, toothpaste, or creams, and do not
burst blisters.

Seek emergency care for a burn that:

- is larger than the child's palm
- involves the face, hands, feet, genitals, or a joint
- goes all the way around a limb
- looks white, brown, charred, or leathery, or is painless — which suggests a
  deep burn
- was caused by chemicals or electricity
- occurs in a baby under 1 year

## Chemical and electrical burns
For a **chemical** burn, protect yourself, brush off any dry powder, then
rinse continuously with running water for at least 20 minutes and seek urgent
care. Take a photograph of the product label if it is safe to do so.

For an **electrical** burn, do not touch the child until the power is switched
off. Electrical injuries can cause internal damage and heart rhythm problems
that are not visible on the skin, so always seek urgent assessment even when
the skin looks unharmed.
