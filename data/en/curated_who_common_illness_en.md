# Common childhood illness — WHO-aligned home care

Summarised from the World Health Organization fact sheets on diarrhoeal
disease and pneumonia (who.int/news-room/fact-sheets) and WHO IMCI guidance. Always seek care for a
child with any general danger sign, and follow a health worker's instructions
for your own child.

## Diarrhoea: oral rehydration and zinc
WHO defines diarrhoea as the passage of 3 or more loose or liquid stools per
day, or more frequently than is normal for that person. It is the third
leading cause of death in children under 5. WHO recognises three clinical
types: acute watery diarrhoea (including cholera), acute bloody diarrhoea
(dysentery), and persistent diarrhoea lasting 14 days or longer.

The dangers are dehydration and undernutrition, so the priorities are fluids,
zinc, and continued feeding.

- give oral rehydration salts (ORS) — a mixture of clean water, salt and sugar
  — after each loose stool; roughly 50–100 ml for a child under 2 years and
  100–200 ml for ages 2–10
- give ORS in small frequent sips; a spoon or syringe helps if the child vomits
- if the child vomits, wait about 10 minutes and then continue more slowly
- give zinc daily for 10–14 days: 20 mg for 6 months and older, 10 mg under
  6 months. WHO reports zinc reduces the duration of an episode by about 25%
  and stool volume by about 30%. Trial evidence since the recommendation
  suggests lower doses (5–10 mg) work as well with less vomiting, so follow
  the dose a local health worker prescribes.
- keep breastfeeding and keep offering nutritious food; do not fast the child
- offer an extra meal each day for two weeks after recovery to regain weight

Avoid sugary drinks, undiluted juice, and fizzy drinks, which can worsen
diarrhoea. Anti-diarrhoeal medicines are not recommended for young children.

WHO grades dehydration by observable signs:

- **severe** — lethargy or unconsciousness, sunken eyes, unable to drink or
  drinking poorly, and a skin pinch that returns very slowly (2 seconds or
  more). This is an emergency needing immediate treatment.
- **some** — restlessness and irritability, sunken eyes, and drinking eagerly
  or appearing very thirsty.
- **none** — not enough signs to classify as dehydration.

Seek urgent care for blood in the stool, any sign of dehydration, diarrhoea
lasting more than 14 days, repeated vomiting, or a child too weak to drink.

Prevention: safe drinking water, improved sanitation, handwashing with soap,
exclusive breastfeeding for the first 6 months, rotavirus vaccination, and good
food hygiene and storage.

## Cough and colds
Young children average 6–8 respiratory infections a year, and cough may persist
for 2–3 weeks after other symptoms resolve.

- offer extra fluids and small frequent feeds
- for a blocked nose, saline drops before feeds can help
- keep the child away from smoke, including cooking smoke and tobacco
- honey may soothe cough in children over 12 months, but never give honey under
  12 months
- over-the-counter cough and cold medicines are not recommended under 6 years
  and offer no proven benefit

Seek care for fast or difficult breathing, chest indrawing, inability to drink,
persistent fever beyond 3 days, or a cough lasting more than 3 weeks.

## Fever: comfort measures
Treat the child's discomfort rather than the number on the thermometer.

- offer fluids frequently; fever increases fluid losses
- dress in light clothing and keep the room comfortable
- paracetamol or ibuprofen may be used for discomfort, dosed by weight and
  according to the package or a health worker's instruction; do not exceed the
  stated frequency
- do not give ibuprofen to a dehydrated child or one with chickenpox without
  advice
- never give aspirin to a child, because of the risk of Reye syndrome
- do not use cold baths, ice, or alcohol rubs

Febrile convulsions occur in some young children. Keep the child safe on their
side, do not put anything in the mouth, time the episode, and seek urgent care
— particularly for a first convulsion or one lasting over 5 minutes.

## Skin: nappy rash, eczema, and infections
- **Nappy rash.** Change nappies frequently, clean gently, allow nappy-free
  time, and apply a barrier cream. A bright red rash with satellite spots that
  persists may be thrush and needs treatment.
- **Eczema.** Use fragrance-free emollients generously and often, bathe in warm
  rather than hot water, and use prescribed treatments as directed. Seek care
  if skin becomes weeping, crusted, or painful, which suggests infection.
- **Urgent.** A rash that does not fade when pressed with a glass, especially
  with fever, drowsiness, or a stiff neck, needs emergency assessment.

## Teething
The first teeth usually appear between 4 and 10 months, though timing varies
widely and later eruption is often normal.

- expect drooling, gum discomfort, and increased chewing
- a chilled — not frozen — teething ring may help, as may gentle gum pressure
- teething does not cause high fever, diarrhoea, or vomiting; look for another
  cause if these occur
- avoid teething gels containing benzocaine or salicylates, and amber necklaces,
  which pose choking and strangulation risks

Begin brushing as soon as the first tooth appears, twice daily with a smear of
fluoride toothpaste.

## Constipation
Stool patterns vary widely; exclusively breastfed babies may pass stool once
every several days and still be normal. Constipation means hard, painful, or
difficult stools.

- for infants under 6 months, seek advice rather than changing feeds yourself
- from 6 months, offer extra water and fibre-containing foods such as fruit,
  vegetables, and pulses
- encourage activity, and for toilet-trained children a relaxed unhurried
  routine after meals

Seek care for constipation in a baby under 8 weeks, blood in stool, vomiting,
abdominal swelling, poor weight gain, or failure to pass meconium in the first
48 hours after birth.
