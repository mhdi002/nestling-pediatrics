# Feeding and nutrition for 2 to 6 year olds (English, curated from NHS and AAP public guidance)

Parent-facing summaries of published guidance for children who have left infancy.
The infant material in this library stops around 24 months; these notes carry it
forward through the toddler and preschool years. General information only — not
individualised medical advice, and national schedules differ.

## Feeding 2–4 years — toddler and preschool meals

From the age of 2 the NHS advises moving your child gradually onto the same foods
as the rest of the family, in the proportions shown in the Eatwell Guide. The
Eatwell Guide does not apply to children under 2, because their nutritional needs
are different. Aim to give your child at least 350ml (12oz) of milk a day, or 2
servings of foods made from milk such as cheese, yoghurt or fromage frais.
Pasteurised whole or semi-skimmed cows', goats' or sheep's milk can be the main
drink from the age of 1; skimmed or 1% fat milk does not contain enough fat and is
not recommended as a main drink for children under 5. Include fruit and vegetables
at every meal, starchy foods such as bread, rice, pasta and potatoes, and each day
either at least 2 portions from plant protein sources or 1 portion from animal
sources. Give at least 1 portion of oily fish a week — boys no more than 4 portions
a week, girls no more than 2. There is no need to add salt to your child's food,
and added sugar should be kept to a minimum, with sugary foods served alongside a
meal rather than as a snack. Talk to your clinician or health visitor if your child
is losing weight, refusing whole food groups for weeks, or has a diagnosed allergy.

Source: NHS, *What to feed young children* and *The Eatwell Guide*.

## Feeding 4–6 years — family meals, drinks and choking safety

By 4 to 6 years most children eat the same meals as everyone else at the table,
built on the Eatwell Guide proportions: plenty of fruit and vegetables, starchy
carbohydrates, some dairy or fortified alternatives, some beans, pulses, fish, eggs
and other proteins, and only small amounts of food high in fat, salt and sugar.
Water and milk are the everyday drinks. Children under 12 months do not need fruit
juice or smoothies at all; if you do give juice to a younger child, dilute it 1
part juice to 10 parts water and serve it with a meal. From age 5 it is fine to
give undiluted fruit juice or a smoothie, but no more than 1 glass (about 150ml) a
day, with a meal. Fizzy drinks are not recommended because they can damage tooth
enamel. Whole nuts, including peanuts, should not be given to children under 5
because of the risk of choking — ground nuts and smooth nut butters are safe
alternatives. Keep young children sitting down and supervised while they eat.
Soft mould-ripened cheeses such as brie and camembert, and unpasteurised cheeses,
are best avoided, and rice drinks should not be given to children under 5.

Source: NHS, *What to feed young children* and *The Eatwell Guide*.

## Portion sizes for a toddler or preschooler

Parents often ask how much food is "enough" at this age, and the honest answer is
that it varies a lot with a child's build and activity level. As a rough guide the
American Academy of Pediatrics says a child between the ages of 1 and 3 years needs
about 40 calories for every inch of height — so a child who is 32 inches tall needs
roughly 1,300 calories a day. A useful rule at the table is that a young child's
serving should be about one-quarter of an adult's serving. Across a day the AAP
suggests roughly 6 servings of grains, 2 to 3 servings of vegetables, 2 to 3
servings of fruit, 2 to 3 servings of dairy and about 2 servings of protein foods,
with beans and pulses counting towards protein. Treat these as starting points, not
targets to enforce: appetite swings from day to day at this age, and what matters is
the pattern across a week and steady growth at routine health checks rather than
what was left on the plate at any single meal.

Source: AAP HealthyChildren, *Serving Sizes for Toddlers*.

## Fussy eating between 2 and 6 years

It is perfectly normal for toddlers and preschoolers to refuse to eat, or even to
taste, a new food. Look at what your child eats across a week rather than at a
single meal. Offer small portions and praise your child for eating, even if they
only eat a little; as long as they are getting foods from the main groups — fruit
and vegetables, starchy foods, dairy, and protein foods — there is usually no need
to worry. Do not force a child to eat something they have rejected, and try not to
use food as a reward, because that can build a negative association with the foods
you want them to enjoy. Keep to about 2 healthy snacks a day so that appetite is
still there at mealtimes. Children learn to eat by copying, so eat the same food
together where you can. Keep offering a rejected food occasionally — tastes change
— and try it prepared a different way, since a child who refuses cooked carrot may
happily eat it raw and grated. Stay calm and keep mealtimes sociable rather than a
battle. Ask your clinician if your child is losing weight, gagging or choking on
textures, or eating an extremely narrow range of foods.

Source: NHS, *Fussy eaters*.

## Vitamin supplements for children under 5

In the UK the government recommends that all children aged 6 months to 5 years are
given a supplement every day containing vitamins A, C and D. Babies who are having
more than 500ml (about a pint) of infant formula a day should not be given these
supplements, because infant formula is already fortified with vitamins A, C and D
and other nutrients. Breastfed babies from birth to 1 year should have a daily
supplement containing 8.5 to 10 micrograms of vitamin D, and children aged 1 to 4
years should have a daily supplement containing 10 micrograms of vitamin D.
Families who qualify for Healthy Start are entitled to free vitamin drops, and a
health visitor can advise where to get them. Recommendations differ between
countries, so follow the schedule your own health service publishes and ask your
clinician before adding any other supplement, especially iron, which should not be
given without advice.

Source: NHS, *Vitamins for children*.
