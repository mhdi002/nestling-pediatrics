# Pediatric skin, growth, and development guidance for parents (English, curated)

Parent-facing summaries inspired by common public pediatric education themes. This is general information only — not a diagnosis. Always contact your child's clinician if you are worried or symptoms worsen.

## Hand, foot, and mouth disease (HFMD)

Hand, foot, and mouth disease is a common viral illness in young children, especially under age 5. It often starts with fever, reduced appetite, and a sore throat. Within a day or two, painful sores may appear inside the mouth (on the tongue, gums, or cheeks) and a rash of flat or slightly raised red spots can show on the palms, soles, and sometimes the buttocks or knees. The spots may look like blisters. The virus spreads through saliva, stool, nose secretions, and fluid from blisters, so good handwashing after diaper changes and before meals helps protect others.

Most children recover in about 7 to 10 days with rest and fluids. Offer cool, soft foods and frequent sips of water or oral rehydration solution if mouth sores make eating painful. Over-the-counter pain or fever medicine may be used only if your clinician has told you it is safe for your child's age and weight. Do not give aspirin to children.

Seek care promptly if your child has signs of dehydration (very dry mouth, no tears, no wet diaper for 8 hours or more in infants, unusual sleepiness), fever lasting more than 3 days, severe headache or neck stiffness, or if symptoms seem much worse than expected. Very young infants and children with weakened immune systems should be evaluated early. This summary does not replace an exam — your clinician can confirm HFMD and rule out other causes.

## Common infant rashes — heat rash, eczema, and viral rash

Baby skin reacts easily to heat, dryness, and infection. **Heat rash (miliaria)** looks like tiny red bumps or clear blisters in sweaty areas — neck, chest, diaper area, or skin folds. It often improves when the room is cooler, clothing is lighter, and skin is kept dry. **Eczema (atopic dermatitis)** causes dry, itchy, red patches, often on the cheeks, arms, or legs. Gentle fragrance-free moisturizer after bathing and avoiding harsh soaps can help; severe itching or cracked skin needs clinician guidance, sometimes including prescribed creams. **Viral rashes** accompany many childhood illnesses — roseola, parvovirus, or hand-foot-and-mouth may cause pink spots that fade when pressed (blanch) or a lacy pattern on the trunk; the rash often appears as fever drops.

Call urgently or go to emergency care if a rash is purple or does not fade when you press a clear glass against it, if the child is very lethargic, breathing fast, has a stiff neck, or fever with a rash that spreads quickly. Same-day clinician advice is wise for rash with fever in a baby under 3 months, widespread blistering, rash around the eyes with swelling, or if your child looks unwell to you. Photos and a brief timeline (when rash started, fever pattern) help your visit. This overview is educational, not a substitute for professional assessment.

## Skin redness, wounds, and blisters — first aid for parents

For minor cuts and scrapes: wash your hands, rinse the wound gently with clean running water, remove visible dirt, pat dry, and apply a thin layer of petroleum jelly or antibiotic ointment if your clinician recommends it. Cover with a clean bandage if the area is likely to get dirty. Change dressings daily or when wet. Most small wounds heal within a week; increasing redness spreading outward, warmth, pus, or red streaks toward the heart suggest infection — contact your clinician.

For blisters from friction or small burns: if the blister is intact and smaller than a coin, leave it alone; the skin underneath protects healing. Do not pop blisters at home. Cool (not ice-cold) running water for 10–20 minutes helps small thermal burns; then cover loosely. Chemical or electrical burns, large burns, burns on face/hands/genitals, or burns that look white or leathery need emergency care.

Go to the ER or call emergency services for heavy bleeding that does not stop after 10 minutes of firm pressure, deep cuts with gaping edges, animal or human bites, embedded objects, serious allergic swelling of face or lips, or any wound your child received without an up-to-date tetanus vaccine status you can confirm. This guide supports home care for mild injuries only — not a diagnosis of severity.

## Fever with rash — red flags for parents

Fever plus rash is common in viral illnesses but some combinations need urgent evaluation. **Seek emergency care now** if your child has a rash that looks like bruising or tiny purple/red spots that do not fade when pressed with a glass, difficulty breathing, extreme drowsiness or confusion, severe headache with vomiting and stiff neck, seizure, or you are unable to wake them normally. These can signal serious infection or bleeding problems requiring immediate treatment.

**Contact your clinician the same day** for fever with rash in an infant under 3 months, fever above 38°C (100.4°F) lasting more than 3 days, rash that is painful or blistering widely, swelling of hands/feet/face with rash, or if your child refuses all fluids. **Monitor closely at home** when fever is mild, the child drinks and urinates normally, and the rash matches a known mild illness (such as viral exanthem after fever breaks) — but trust your instinct; if they look "not themselves," call anyway.

Keep a fever log (times and temperatures), note when the rash appeared relative to fever, and list medicines given. Do not use aspirin for fever in children. Antibiotics do not treat typical viral rashes unless prescribed. This list highlights warning signs; it cannot tell you which illness your child has — only a clinician can.

## Photographs of your child's skin — what to tell the clinician

Many clinicians now review photos before or during visits. Good photos help; poor ones can mislead. Take pictures in natural daylight near a window, without flash when possible — flash can wash out redness. Include one wide shot showing where the rash sits on the body and close-ups in focus. Place a ruler or coin beside a lesion for size reference. Capture whether spots fade when you press the side of a clear glass firmly for a few seconds (non-blanching vs blanching).

Write a short note with: child's age, date and time photos taken, when symptoms started, fever highest temperature and timing, itching or pain level, new medicines or foods, sick contacts, and what has helped or worsened symptoms. Avoid filters or heavy editing. If the rash changes hour to hour, take a series labeled with times.

Photos cannot replace an in-person exam when your child is ill — send images through your clinic's approved secure channel, not public social media. If non-blanching rash, breathing trouble, or lethargy is present, do not wait for a photo reply; seek urgent care. This guidance helps you communicate clearly; it is not remote diagnosis.

## Growth charts — what parents should know about centiles

Growth charts plot weight, length or height, and head circumference (in infants) against age. The curved lines are **centiles** (percentiles): for example, the 50th centile is the middle of the reference population, not a grade to "pass." Healthy children can track near the 5th, 50th, or 95th centile if they **follow their own curve** over time. A single point matters less than the pattern across visits.

Your clinician compares your child to sex-specific WHO or national reference charts. **Crossing centiles sharply** — for example, dropping from the 50th to the 10th over two visits — may prompt checking feeding, illness, or other causes. **Parallel tracking** two centiles below or above average is often normal for that child, especially if birth size and family height pattern fit. Preterm infants need corrected age and sometimes specialized preterm charts — do not read a preterm baby on a term chart without clinician guidance.

Home scales vary; clinic measurements are more reliable. Do not restrict food or force extra feeds to "reach" a higher centile unless your clinician advises a plan. Bring your growth booklet or app records to each visit and ask what your child's trend means for them. This explains general concepts; your child's plan must come from their clinician.

## Speech and language milestones — birth to 24 months (brief)

Early communication builds in steps; ranges vary widely. **0–3 months:** cooing, crying for needs, calming to familiar voices, social smiling by about 2 months. **4–6 months:** laughing, babbling with consonants ("ba," "ga"), turning to sounds, exploring voice pitch. **7–12 months:** babbling chains, responding to name, gestures like waving or pointing, first words such as "mama" or "dada" with meaning often near 12 months (some earlier or later). **13–18 months:** several single words, pointing to show interest, following simple one-step commands ("give me the ball"). **19–24 months:** two-word phrases ("more milk"), naming body parts, growing vocabulary; strangers understand some speech by age 2 though pronunciation is often unclear.

Talk, read, and respond to your child's sounds throughout the day — repetition and face-to-face interaction matter more than screens. Seek pediatric advice if by 12 months there is no babbling, no gestures, no response to name, or loss of words/skills; by 18 months no single words; by 24 months no two-word phrases — or anytime you feel concerned. Hearing checks are part of speech evaluation. Age-appropriate screening tools (such as ASQ communication items) can structure your concerns but do not replace clinical assessment. Milestones describe typical development, not guarantees — your clinician interprets them for your child.
