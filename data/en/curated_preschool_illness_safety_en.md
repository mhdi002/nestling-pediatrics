# Common illness and injury prevention for 2 to 6 year olds (English, curated from NHS and WHO public guidance)

Parent-facing guidance on the illnesses and injury risks that come up most in the
toddler and preschool years. This is general information, not a diagnosis: if you
are worried about your child, or your child looks unwell to you, contact your
clinician or local emergency service rather than waiting.

## Fever in a young child — what to do and when to get help

NHS guidance takes a high temperature to be 38C or more, and says it usually settles
within 1 to 4 days. At home, give your child plenty of fluids, offer food if they
want it, watch for signs of dehydration, and check on them during the night. You can
give paracetamol or ibuprofen if your child is distressed, following the age and
dose instructions on the packaging. Do not undress your child or sponge them down to
cool them, and do not cover them in too many layers. Get urgent advice (in the UK,
call 111) if a baby under 3 months has a temperature of 38C or higher, if a baby
aged 3 to 6 months has a temperature of 39C or higher, or if a fever at any age has
lasted 5 days or more. Call the emergency number (999 in the UK) or go to an
emergency department for a rash that does not fade when you press a glass against
it, a stiff neck or dislike of bright light, unusually cold hands and feet,
difficulty breathing, a child who is very drowsy or hard to wake, skin that looks
blue, grey, pale or blotchy, a first fit, or a child who is extremely agitated. Trust
your judgement: a fever number matters far less than how ill the child looks.

Source: NHS, *Fever in children*.

## Diarrhoea and vomiting in a young child

WHO's approach to childhood diarrhoea rests on two things: replacing the fluid and
salts that are being lost, and continuing to feed. Oral rehydration solution (ORS) —
a mixture of clean water, salt and sugar — is the mainstay, and WHO recommends a 10
to 14 day supplementary treatment course of dispersible zinc tablets alongside it,
which reduces the length of the episode by about 25% and stool volume by about 30%.
Keep giving nutrient-rich food, including breast milk if your child is still
breastfeeding, during the illness rather than starving the gut. Contact a health
worker in particular for diarrhoea that persists, for blood in the stool, or if
there are signs of dehydration. WHO describes severe dehydration as a child who is
lethargic or unconscious, has sunken eyes, is unable to drink or drinks poorly, or
whose skin pinch goes back very slowly (2 seconds or more) — that needs urgent
medical care, not home treatment. In the UK, NHS advice is to keep a child away from
school or nursery until they have not been sick and have had no diarrhoea for at
least 2 days (48 hours).

Source: WHO, *Diarrhoeal disease* fact sheet; NHS, *Is my child too ill for school?*.

## When a 2 to 6 year old is too ill for nursery or school

NHS guidance is that it is usually fine to send a child in with a slight cough or a
common cold as long as they do not have a high temperature, and that a child with a
high temperature should be kept off until it goes away. For chickenpox, keep them
off until all the spots have crusted over, which is usually about 5 days after the
spots first appeared. For vomiting or diarrhoea, stay away until they have not been
sick or had diarrhoea for at least 2 days (48 hours). A child with scarlet fever can
go back 24 hours after starting antibiotics. With impetigo, keep them off until all
the sores have crusted over and healed, or for 48 hours after starting antibiotic
treatment. Conjunctivitis does not need time off unless the child feels very unwell,
hand, foot and mouth disease does not need time off if the child is well enough to
go, and there is no need to keep a child off school for head lice. These are UK
rules of thumb; your nursery, school or local health service may have its own
policy, and some infections are notifiable where you live.

Source: NHS, *Is my child too ill for school?*.

## Injury prevention for 2 to 6 year olds — water, burns and roads

Drowning is one of the largest single risks to a child of this age. WHO reports that
children under 5 account for nearly a quarter of all drowning deaths and that the
highest drowning rates of any age group are among children aged 0 to 4 years, and it
identifies drowning as the fourth leading cause of death for children aged 1 to 4.
The prevention measures WHO recommends are physical and simple: covering wells,
using doorway barriers and playpens, fencing swimming pools, providing supervised
childcare for pre-school children, and teaching school-age children basic swimming,
water safety and safe rescue skills. On burns, WHO stresses that burns are
preventable and that a major risk to children is improper adult supervision; it
recommends improving cookstove design so that stoves are stable and children cannot
reach them, enclosing fires and limiting the height of open flames in the home, and
using fire-retardant fabrics for children's sleepwear. On the road, WHO reports that
the use of child restraints can lead to a 71% reduction in deaths among infants,
that wearing a seat-belt can reduce the risk of death among vehicle occupants by up
to 50%, and that correct helmet use can reduce the risk of death in a crash by more
than 6 times and the risk of brain injury by up to 74%. Use a restraint appropriate
to your child's size on every journey, and follow the fitting rules for your country.

Source: WHO fact sheets on *Drowning*, *Burns* and *Road traffic injuries*.
