# Infant and young child feeding by age (English, curated from WHO/CDC public guidance)

Parent-facing summaries based on open WHO infant feeding fact sheets and CDC nutrition guidance. General information only — not individualized medical advice. Sources: WHO infant and young child feeding; WHO complementary feeding guideline 2023; CDC infant nutrition.

## Newborn feeding — first 2 weeks

In the first hours after birth, most healthy term newborns benefit from skin-to-skin contact and early breastfeeding when possible. Colostrum (early milk) is small in volume but rich in antibodies. Newborns typically feed 8–12 times per 24 hours, including overnight. Wet diapers usually increase from about 1 on day 1 toward 6 or more by day 5; stool patterns vary (yellow seedy stools are common in breastfed babies). Formula-fed infants use iron-fortified infant formula prepared per label instructions — never dilute extra water into formula. Do not give honey, cow's milk as main drink, juice, or solid foods in the newborn period. Seek urgent care for poor feeding with lethargy, fewer than expected wet diapers after day 4, yellow skin/eyes before 24 hours of age, fever in a baby under 3 months, or if you cannot wake the baby for feeds.

## Feeding 0–3 months — breastmilk or formula only

From birth through roughly 4 months, infants should receive only breast milk or iron-fortified infant formula (plus prescribed vitamin drops if your clinician recommends). WHO recommends exclusive breastfeeding for about the first 6 months when possible — meaning no water, juice, cereal, or other solids. Formula-fed babies need appropriate amounts of standard infant formula; follow preparation and storage safety. Growth spurts may temporarily increase hunger. Vitamin D supplementation is commonly advised for breastfed infants per national guidance. Do not introduce complementary solids at 0.2 months (about 1 week) — the gut and kidneys are not ready; milk alone meets needs. Watch for adequate weight gain at well visits rather than comparing to other babies daily.

## Feeding 4–5 months — still milk only, prepare for solids

Most infants still need only breast milk or iron-fortified formula through 5 months. Some families notice increased night waking or chewing motions — these do not by themselves mean solids must start early. Continue responsive feeding: offer milk when hungry, stop when satisfied. If exclusively breastfed, discuss iron supplementation timing with your clinician (often considered around 4 months in some guidelines until iron-rich foods are established). Practice good oral hygiene even before teeth. Around 5–6 months, watch developmental readiness signs for solids: good head control, sitting with support, diminishing tongue-thrust, interest in food. Premature infants may use corrected age for readiness — ask your clinician.

## Starting solids at 6 months — WHO complementary feeding

At about 6 months (180 days), infants usually need complementary foods in addition to breast milk or formula because energy and iron needs exceed milk alone. Continue breastfeeding on demand while adding solids. Start with single-ingredient iron-rich foods: iron-fortified infant cereal, pureed meat, lentils, or beans. Offer one new food every few days to watch for allergy. Texture progresses from smooth purees toward mashed and soft finger foods by 9–12 months. Avoid added salt and sugar. Common allergens (peanut, egg) may be introduced in age-appropriate forms unless your clinician advises otherwise. Honey is unsafe before 12 months (botulism risk). Choking hazards include whole grapes, nuts, popcorn, and hard raw vegetables — modify shape and size. Milk remains a major calorie source through the first year.

## Feeding 7–9 months — expanding variety

Offer 2–3 meals per day plus breast milk or formula. Include vegetables, fruits, grains, and protein foods across the week. Encourage self-feeding with soft pieces when safe. Cup introduction can begin with small sips of water at meals. Continue iron-rich foods daily — breast milk alone provides limited iron as intake grows. Limit fruit juice; whole fruit is preferred. Responsive feeding means following hunger and fullness cues rather than forcing empty plates. If growth or anemia screening shows concerns, your clinician may adjust diet or supplements. Cultural family foods can be adapted to baby-safe textures without heavy salt or sugar.

## Feeding 10–12 months — family foods emerging

Many infants take 3 meals plus snacks while breastfeeding or using formula. Finger foods support motor skills: soft banana, avocado, scrambled egg, shredded meat, cooked pasta. Whole cow's milk as a drink is generally not the main milk before 12 months in many guidelines — continue breast milk or formula unless your clinician advises transition. Continue avoiding choking hazards and honey. Mealtimes are social learning — eat together when possible. Appetite varies day to day; trust the child's cues within healthy options. Dental care: brush emerging teeth with rice-grain smear of fluoride toothpaste if recommended locally.

## Feeding 12–24 months — toddler patterns

After the first birthday, many children transition toward family meals with modifications. Whole milk (if not breastfeeding heavily) often becomes the main milk drink in appropriate amounts; limit to about 16–24 oz (480–720 ml) daily so solids are not displaced. Offer varied fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and protein. Continue breastfeeding if desired — WHO supports continued breastfeeding to 2 years and beyond alongside solids. Toddler portions are small (e.g., 1–2 tablespoons per year of age per food group as a rough starting point). Picky phases are common; repeated neutral exposure helps. Limit sugary drinks and highly processed snacks. Iron-rich foods remain important as rapid brain growth continues.

## Formula preparation and food safety basics

Always wash hands before feeding. Prepare powdered formula with safe water at label temperature; use within recommended time after mixing. Discard unfinished bottles after a feed if your clinician advises (bacteria grow in warm milk). Store breast milk in clean containers with dated labels per safe-storage charts. Reheat gently — avoid microwaving bottles unevenly. For solids, cook meats and eggs thoroughly; refrigerate leftovers promptly. When baby-led weaning, ensure soft shapes and supervised meals. Travel and childcare require clear instructions on allergies and choking-safe foods. These practices reduce infection and support healthy growth alongside regular well-child visits.
