# Common breastfeeding challenges

Adapted from *Infant and young child feeding: model chapter for textbooks for
medical students and allied health professionals, second edition*
(World Health Organization, 2025), available under CC BY-NC-SA 3.0 IGO.
Parent-facing guidance — a midwife, lactation counsellor or health worker
should assess an individual mother and baby.

## Nipple pain while breastfeeding
Nipple pain during breastfeeding is not normal. When a mother has nipple pain
it is very likely the baby is not attaching properly to the breast, even if
there are no other obvious signs of poor attachment. A baby who is not
attached properly may also not be transferring milk effectively.

Poor attachment can damage the nipple, from a mild graze to deep fissures
across the tip or around the base. The nipple may look discoloured, squashed
from side to side at the end of a feed, or show a white pressure line across
the tip.

The usual remedy is help with positioning and attachment rather than creams or
resting the breast. Once the baby is well attached and the nipple is no longer
being damaged, pain decreases and the nipple heals quickly.

Nipple pain that persists despite good attachment suggests an anatomical or
infectious cause and needs to be investigated.

## Flat, inverted or large nipples
Nipples vary widely in shape and usually this does not affect the ability to
breastfeed. Very flat, inverted, large or long nipples can make attachment
harder.

Most nipples that look flat are protractile — if pulled out with the fingers
they stretch, just as they stretch in a baby's mouth. A baby should have no
difficulty feeding from a flat but protractile nipple. An inverted nipple
retracts when stimulated and is harder to attach to; protractility often
improves during pregnancy and in the first weeks after birth.

What helps:

- reassurance during pregnancy: most babies can suckle from nipples of any shape
- no special nipple preparation during pregnancy — it is unnecessary and can
  undermine a mother's confidence
- skilled breastfeeding support soon after birth, and early help with
  positioning and attachment
- plenty of skin-to-skin contact near the breast, letting the baby find their
  own way to the breast
- trying different positions until the baby can attach
- expressing a little milk into the baby's mouth and touching their lips to
  trigger the rooting reflex, encouraging a wider gape
- expressing milk and feeding by cup while the baby is still learning
- avoiding bottles and dummies, which can change how a baby suckles

## A crying baby
Crying is normal and tends to increase until it peaks at around 6–8 weeks,
when many babies cry for 2–3 hours a day for no apparent reason. Most babies
have a period of crying in the late afternoon or evening when they may want to
feed very frequently and are hard to console for some hours.

A *sudden* change — a baby who was settled and is now inconsolable — is
different from this normal pattern and should be assessed rather than assumed
to be colic.

## Feed refusal
Babies refuse the breast for many reasons. In the newborn period this can be
caused by:

- sedation from pain relief given to the mother during labour
- pain from bruising or other birth trauma
- stiffness in the neck or body from position in the uterus
- difficulty attaching, or frustration from poor milk transfer
- getting used to the different sucking action a bottle requires

In older infants, refusal can be caused by pain from illness such as oral
thrush, ear infection, gastroenteritis or reflux, and can also indicate
mastitis, which changes the flavour of the milk.

Pressing on the back of a baby's head or forcing them onto the breast makes
refusal worse — babies resist by pulling away.

What helps: identify and treat the cause, reduce separation, limit how many
people are handling the baby for a time, keep the baby close with frequent
skin-to-skin contact, offer the breast whenever the baby shows interest, feed
in a comfortable position, express milk into the baby's mouth to encourage
suckling, and feed expressed milk by cup rather than bottle until the baby
returns to the breast.

## Engorgement
Engorgement usually happens when milk production increases quickly in the
first days after birth, and is made worse when milk is not removed
effectively. Blood flow to the breasts also rises, causing swelling that can
itself inhibit milk flow. It is normally temporary.

Delayed initiation of breastfeeding, infrequent or scheduled feeds, poor
attachment, separation from the newborn, and ineffective suckling all make
engorgement more likely and more severe.

Both breasts are usually affected. The main symptom is pain: breasts feel
swollen, hard and tight, the skin looks shiny and diffusely red, and the
nipples may not protract, making attachment harder. A mother may have a fever
in the first 24 hours, which usually settles.

Relieving engorgement — the mainstay is removing milk from the breast:

- apply warm compresses or take a warm shower before feeding or expressing to
  help the milk flow
- breastfeed as frequently as the baby needs, if the baby can attach well
- if the baby cannot attach effectively, express by hand or pump a few times;
  once the breasts soften and the baby can attach, increase feeding frequency
- reverse pressure softening can reduce swelling and help the nipple stand out
- therapeutic breast massage, and lymphatic drainage massage from the nipple
  towards the armpit, can relieve swelling
- use cool compresses *after* feeding or expressing to reduce swelling

## Oversupply
Oversupply (hyperlactation) is producing much more milk than the baby needs.
It often comes with an overactive milk ejection reflex and fast flow, which is
uncomfortable for both mother and baby in the short term. Left unaddressed, a
fast independent flow can stop the baby learning to actively remove milk and
interfere with establishing feeding.

## When to seek help urgently
Seek prompt assessment for:

- a red, painful, swollen area of breast with fever or flu-like symptoms,
  which may be mastitis
- a painful lump that does not settle after feeding and massage
- nipple pain that persists despite good attachment
- a baby who is feeding poorly, has fewer wet nappies than expected, or is not
  gaining weight
- any sudden change from a baby's normal feeding or crying pattern
