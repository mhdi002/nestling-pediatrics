# Infant and child sleep guidance by age (English, curated from AAP/WHO public themes)

Educational summaries inspired by American Academy of Pediatrics safe sleep recommendations and common pediatric sleep education. Not a substitute for personalized medical advice.

## Safe sleep for newborns and infants — ABCs

For the highest-risk SIDS period, place babies on their back for every sleep on a firm, flat sleep surface in a safety-approved crib, bassinet, or play yard — no soft bedding, pillows, bumper pads, or stuffed toys. Room-sharing (baby's sleep space in parents' room) without bed-sharing is recommended for at least the first 6 months and ideally the first year in many guidelines. Avoid overheating; dress in one light layer more than an adult would wear. Pacifiers at sleep onset may reduce SIDS risk once breastfeeding is established — do not force if refused. Avoid smoke exposure during pregnancy and after birth. Tummy time while awake and supervised strengthens muscles. Car seats and swings are not routine sleep locations. If the baby rolls both ways independently, continue back placement at sleep onset.

## Sleep 0–3 months — frequent waking is normal

Newborns sleep 14–17 hours per 24 hours in irregular chunks, waking every 2–4 hours to feed. Day/night confusion is common in the first weeks; keep days brighter and slightly more active, nights calmer and dim. Most infants cannot self-soothe consistently yet — responding to hunger and discomfort is appropriate. Contact your clinician if you cannot wake the baby for feeds, breathing looks labored, or sleepiness is extreme with poor feeding. Swaddling may help some infants if hips have room to move and swaddle stops once rolling begins. Never use weighted blankets or sleep positioners marketed to prevent reflux without clinician guidance.

## Sleep 4–7 months — longer stretches may appear

Total sleep often 12–15 hours including naps. Some infants sleep 5–8 hour stretches at night, others still wake to feed — both can be normal with adequate weight gain. Establish a simple bedtime routine: bath, feed, book, bed. Put down drowsy but awake when possible to practice self-settling, without cry-it-out unless you choose a supported method with your clinician. Nap schedule may be 3–4 naps merging toward 2–3. Teething and illness disrupt sleep temporarily. Regression at developmental leaps is common. Continue safe sleep environment even when sitting or rolling.

## Sleep 8–12 months — separation anxiety and mobility

Many babies take 2 naps (morning and afternoon) totaling 2–3 hours plus 10–12 hours night sleep, though ranges vary widely. Separation anxiety can increase night waking — brief reassuring check-ins without long playtime help. Crawling and standing in crib may delay sleep onset; ensure crib mattress is lowest setting. Avoid introducing bottles in bed once teeth erupt to reduce dental decay risk if milk pools. Travel and illness cause temporary setbacks. Persistent snoring, pauses in breathing, or extreme mouth breathing warrant clinician evaluation for sleep-disordered breathing.

## Sleep 12–24 months — one nap transition

Toddlers often need 11–14 hours total sleep. Morning nap may drop between 15–18 months for many children, leaving one afternoon nap of 1–3 hours. Bedtime resistance and boundary testing are developmental. Consistent limits, predictable routines, and dark cool sleep environment support rest. Limit screens before bed per AAP guidance. Nightmares and night terrors can appear — comfort without extensive stimulation. Keep crib until climbing out becomes unsafe, then transition to toddler bed with room safety check. Caffeine and chocolate are unnecessary for toddlers.

## When to seek help for infant sleep problems

Talk to your clinician if snoring is loud nightly, you observe breathing pauses or gasping, the child is excessively sleepy during awake periods, growth falters, or night waking is accompanied by pain signs (arch, scream with feeds, blood in stool). Mental health support helps parents with severe sleep deprivation. Safe sleep rules still apply during illness — elevate head only if clinician advises specific positions. Sleep training choices are family decisions; avoid unsafe practices (car sleep unsupervised, couch sharing, propping bottles). This guide supports general education, not diagnosis of sleep disorders.
