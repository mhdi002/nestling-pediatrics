# WHO infant and young child feeding

Summarised from the World Health Organization fact sheet "Infant and young
child feeding" (who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/infant-and-young-child-feeding)
and the WHO fact sheet on malnutrition. General information for parents — a
health worker should advise on an individual child, especially where growth
faltering or illness is present.

## WHO breastfeeding recommendations
WHO recommends that:

- all infants initiate breastfeeding within 1 hour of birth
- infants are breastfed exclusively — fed only breast milk — for the first
  6 months of life
- nutritionally adequate, safe complementary foods are introduced from
  6 months
- breastfeeding continues up to 2 years of age or beyond

WHO estimates that nearly 400 000 deaths among children under 5 could be
prevented each year through optimal breastfeeding practices. Globally only
about 47% of infants aged 0–6 months are exclusively breastfed.

Exclusive breastfeeding means no other food or drink, not even water. Vitamin
supplements, oral rehydration salts, and prescribed medicines do not break
exclusive breastfeeding.

## Meal frequency for complementary feeding
From 6 months breast milk alone no longer meets a child's energy and nutrient
needs. WHO advises:

- 6–8 months: 2–3 meals per day
- 9–23 months: 3–4 meals per day
- plus 1–2 nutritious snacks per day as needed
- continued frequent breastfeeding through age 2 and beyond

Start with small amounts and gradually increase the consistency and variety of
food as the child grows, adapting to their abilities and needs.

## Responsive feeding and hygiene
WHO's complementary feeding principles include:

- practising responsive feeding — recognising and answering hunger and fullness
  cues rather than aiming to empty a bowl
- feeding slowly and patiently, encouraging but never forcing
- maintaining good hygiene and safe food handling, since contaminated food is a
  leading cause of diarrhoea
- continuing to offer a refused food on later occasions, as acceptance often
  takes repeated exposure

## Signs a baby is getting enough milk
Practical indicators that feeding is going well in the early weeks:

- at least 6 heavy wet nappies in 24 hours once the milk supply is established
- regular stools, typically soft and yellow in breastfed babies after the first
  week
- steady weight gain after the initial newborn weight loss, with birth weight
  usually regained by around 2 weeks
- 8 or more feeds in 24 hours in the newborn period, including at night
- audible or visible swallowing during feeds

Most babies lose up to 7–10% of birth weight in the first days. Loss beyond
10%, or failure to regain birth weight by 2 weeks, needs review by a health
worker.

## Foods and drinks to avoid in the first years
- honey before 12 months, because of the risk of infant botulism
- whole cow's milk as a main drink before 12 months, though small amounts in
  cooked food are fine
- added salt and added sugar; sugary drinks and fruit juice are not recommended
  for infants
- whole nuts, whole grapes, popcorn, and other hard round foods, which are
  choking hazards under about 4 years — cut grapes lengthways into quarters
- unpasteurised milk, and undercooked eggs or meat
- tea and coffee, which reduce iron absorption

Always supervise a child while eating and keep them seated.

## Undernutrition: WHO definitions
WHO describes undernutrition using three growth indicators:

- **wasting** — low weight-for-height, indicating recent or severe weight loss,
  resulting from insufficient food intake or infectious disease. Moderately or
  severely wasted children face an increased risk of death.
- **stunting** — low height-for-age, resulting from chronic or recurrent
  undernutrition, which prevents children reaching their physical and cognitive
  potential.
- **underweight** — low weight-for-age. An underweight child may be stunted,
  wasted, or both.

WHO estimated in 2022 that 149 million children under 5 were stunted and
45 million were wasted. Nearly half of all deaths in children under 5 are
linked to undernutrition.

Mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) is used in children aged 6–59 months as a
screening measure: below 115 mm indicates severe acute malnutrition needing
urgent treatment, and 115 mm to under 125 mm indicates moderate acute
malnutrition. Bilateral pitting oedema of both feet indicates severe acute
malnutrition regardless of measurements and always requires immediate care.

A child crossing downward through growth chart centiles over time needs
assessment even when a single measurement still looks acceptable.

## Micronutrients: iron, vitamin D, vitamin A, zinc
- **Iron.** Needs rise sharply after 6 months as birth stores are used up.
  Offer iron-rich foods daily — meat, fish, eggs, pulses, and iron-fortified
  cereals. Preterm and low-birth-weight infants often need supplementation.
- **Vitamin D.** Breast milk is low in vitamin D. Many national guidelines
  recommend a daily supplement of about 400 IU (10 micrograms) for breastfed
  infants. Follow local guidance.
- **Vitamin A.** WHO recommends periodic vitamin A supplementation for children
  6–59 months in settings where deficiency is a public health problem.
- **Zinc.** WHO recommends zinc during diarrhoea — see the diarrhoea guidance.

Do not give supplements beyond those advised for your child; some vitamins are
harmful in excess.
