# Daily routines for 2 to 6 year olds — teeth, toilet training, screens and activity (English, curated from NHS, WHO and AAP public guidance)

Parent-facing guidance on the everyday routines that come up most in the toddler and
preschool years. General information only — not individualised medical advice, and
national recommendations differ.

## Toothbrushing and dental care for 2 to 6 year olds

NHS guidance is to start brushing as soon as the first milk tooth comes through, and
to keep brushing at least twice a day for about 2 minutes, with one of those times
being the last thing at night before bed. The amount and strength of toothpaste
changes with age: up to 3 years use a smear of children's fluoride toothpaste
containing no less than 1,000ppm of fluoride; from 3 to 6 years use a pea-sized
amount of children's fluoride toothpaste containing 1,000ppm to 1,500ppm fluoride;
from 7 years use toothpaste containing between 1,350ppm and 1,500ppm. Young children
need an adult to brush for them or to supervise and finish the job, because they
cannot yet clean their own teeth thoroughly. Take your child to the dentist when
their first milk teeth appear, or before they are 12 months old — early visits are
partly so the child gets used to the surroundings and partly so problems are caught
early. NHS dental care for children is free. Keep sugary food and drink to mealtimes
rather than snacks, and avoid fizzy drinks, which can damage tooth enamel.

Source: NHS, *Taking care of children's teeth*.

## Toilet training a toddler — when and how to start

NHS guidance says almost all children can be potty trained between 18 months and
two-and-a-half years old, and that you do not have to wait for your child to show
readiness signs before you begin. Some children do show them — noticing when they
are wet or need changing, or when they are having a wee or poo, being able to sit
down on a potty and get back up again, or a gap of at least an hour between wetting
— but plenty of children never do, and that is completely normal. Choose a stretch
of time when you can give it attention and when little else is changing: avoid
starting around a house move, a new baby, or the first weeks at nursery. Tell your
child what is going to change, switch to pants during the day so they can feel when
they are wet or dry, and encourage regular visits to the potty or toilet at natural
points — on waking, after meals, before bed. Praise effort with clapping, stickers
or a chart. Build the surrounding skills gradually: wiping, flushing, hand-washing,
pulling clothes up and down, which is easier in simple clothes without zips and
buttons. Get the daytime working before you take the nappy off at night. Accidents
are part of learning; if your child is not engaging at all, put it aside for a few
weeks and try again rather than forcing it.

Source: NHS, *Potty training your child* and *How to potty train*.

## Screen time for 2 to 6 year olds

WHO's guidance for children under 5 says that for a 2 year old sedentary screen time
should be no more than 1 hour, and less is better; screen time is not recommended at
all for children under 2. The same 1 hour ceiling, with "less is better", applies at
3 and 4 years. The American Academy of Pediatrics gives a matching figure for the
preschool years, recommending that children aged 2 to 5 be limited to about 1 hour a
day of high-quality programming, watched together with a parent or carer where
possible so an adult can explain what is on screen and set limits. The AAP also
advises keeping meals screen-free and avoiding screens for the hour before bedtime,
and keeping screens out of the bedroom. When a young child is sitting still, WHO
suggests that time is better spent being read to or told stories with a caregiver.
These are ceilings for recreational screen use, not a target to reach, and what is
on the screen and whether an adult is watching alongside matter as much as the
number of minutes.

Source: WHO, *guidelines on physical activity, sedentary behaviour and sleep for children under 5 years of age*; AAP policy statement *Media and Young Minds*.

## Physical activity and active play for 2 to 6 year olds

WHO recommends that 1 to 2 year olds spend at least 180 minutes across the day in a
variety of physical activities at any intensity, and that 3 and 4 year olds spend at
least 180 minutes in a variety of physical activities of which at least 60 minutes
is moderate- to vigorous-intensity. NHS guidance for under-5s who can walk says the
same: preschoolers should spend at least 180 minutes (3 hours) a day doing a variety
of physical activities spread throughout the day, including active and outdoor play,
and children under 5 should not be inactive for long periods except when they are
asleep — long stretches in a buggy, a car seat or in front of a television are not
good for a child's health and development. From age 5 the NHS target changes shape:
children and young people aged 5 to 18 should aim for an average of at least 60
minutes of moderate or vigorous intensity physical activity a day across the week,
across a variety of types and intensities that develop movement skills, muscles and
bones, and should break up long periods of sitting still. None of this needs to be
organised sport — running, climbing, dancing, scooting and outdoor play all count.

Source: WHO, *guidelines on physical activity, sedentary behaviour and sleep for children under 5 years of age*; NHS physical activity guidelines for under-5s and for children and young people.
