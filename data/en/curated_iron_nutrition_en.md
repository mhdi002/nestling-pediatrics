# Iron and key micronutrients in infancy (English, curated from WHO/CDC/AAP public guidance)

Educational summary on iron needs in infants and young children. Follow your clinician for dosing and blood tests.

## Why iron matters in infancy

Iron supports hemoglobin, which carries oxygen to the brain and muscles. Rapid growth in infancy and toddlerhood increases iron demand. Iron deficiency can cause anemia, fatigue, poor feeding, and in severe or prolonged cases may affect development. Full-term infants are born with iron stores from pregnancy; these stores typically last about 4–6 months. Breast milk provides highly absorbable iron but in modest amounts — adequate for many exclusively breastfed infants early on, but often insufficient alone after about 6 months without complementary iron-rich foods or supplements. Premature infants have lower stores and higher needs. Never give adult iron supplements or excess doses — iron overdose is toxic.

## Iron for breastfed infants — supplementation timing

Exclusively breastfed term infants often need iron supplementation starting around 4 months until iron-rich complementary foods are established well, per many pediatric guidelines (AAP historically 4 months; some regions align with 6 months if solids rich in iron start promptly). Dose depends on weight and local policy — common examples are 1 mg/kg/day elemental iron up to a maximum daily dose prescribed by your clinician. Continue while breastfeeding if complementary diet lacks adequate iron. If formula is introduced, iron-fortified formula contributes substantially — adjust supplements with clinician guidance to avoid excess. Premature infants frequently need earlier and higher-dose iron. Blood hemoglobin/ferritin screening at 9–12 months is common in well-child care.

## Iron for formula-fed and mixed-fed infants

Standard infant formulas in many countries are iron-fortified and usually meet iron needs without extra drops unless deficiency is found on labs. Do not switch to low-iron formula without clinician advice. When mixing breast and formula feeding, calculate total iron intake with your clinician. Cow's milk as main drink before 12 months can displace iron-rich foods and irritate the gut — another reason to delay large volumes of cow's milk. When starting solids at 6 months, prioritize iron-fortified cereal, pureed meats, beans, lentils, tofu, and dark leafy greens in baby-safe forms.

## Vitamin D, vitamin K, and other supplements

Vitamin K is routinely given at birth to prevent bleeding disorder of the newborn. Vitamin D supports bone health; breastfed infants commonly receive 400 IU (10 mcg) daily vitamin D from the first days of life per many guidelines, including formula-fed babies until about 1 liter formula daily. Vitamin A supplementation may be recommended in deficiency-prone regions per WHO programs. Multivitamins are not automatically needed for all healthy children — targeted supplementation based on diet and risk is preferred. Fluoride supplementation depends on local water fluoride and dental guidance. Always use infant-specific products and measured dispensers; store supplements locked away from children.

## Recognizing possible iron deficiency — when to test

Signs that warrant clinician evaluation include pallor, irritability, poor weight gain, pica (eating non-food items), or dietary patterns very low in iron (excessive milk without solids in toddlers). Routine screening often occurs at 9–12 months or if risk factors exist (prematurity, low birth weight, exclusive breastfeeding beyond 6 months without iron foods). Treatment may be oral iron with follow-up labs. Do not treat presumed anemia with supplements alone without diagnosis — other causes exist. Pair iron foods with vitamin C sources (citrus, berries) to enhance absorption; avoid giving iron with large calcium-only feeds simultaneously if possible.
